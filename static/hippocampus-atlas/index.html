Spatially tuned cells

Theta phase precession

As the animal crosses a place field, the cell fires at progressively earlier theta phases (O'Keefe & Recce 1993). Each theta cycle therefore compresses a sequence of upcoming places into ~125 ms, a time scale suited to spike-timing-dependent plasticity.

Pattern separation (dentate gyrus)

Two entorhinal patterns with a given overlap are projected onto 2,000 model granule cells with random connections; only the most strongly driven fraction fires (lateral inhibition). Sparser codes push similar inputs further apart: points below the diagonal mean separation.

Pattern completion (CA3)

A 144-unit recurrent network stores three patterns in its recurrent weights, as CA3 recurrent collaterals are thought to. A partial or noisy cue settles into the nearest stored memory (an attractor). Push degradation past ~40% and recall fails or lands on the wrong memory.

The long axis: dorsal versus ventral

In rodents the hippocampus curves from dorsal (septal) to ventral (temporal). In humans these correspond to the posterior body/tail and anterior head. Function, connections and gene expression change along this axis (Fanselow & Dong 2010; Strange et al. 2014).

Space & time

Hippocampal neurons respond to places, moments, routes, laps, objects, other animals and even ideas. This view collects those functional cell types, asks what single principle could produce all of them, and shows how the same machinery may let us travel mentally into the past and the future.

Featured theory · Raju, Guntupalli, Zhou, Wendelken, Lázaro-Gredilla & George · Science Advances 10, eadm8470 (2024)

Space is a latent sequence

The paper argues that the brain's map of space is not built from coordinates. It emerges when an animal learns the higher-order sequences of what it senses and does. Animals have no GPS. They only receive egocentric sensations, and those are aliased: the same view can occur in many places. To predict what comes next, a learner must split one sensation into several latent states according to the sequence it appears in. Once those states are learned, they behave like places.

The model is a clone-structured causal graph (CSCG), an action-conditioned hidden Markov model in which each observation owns a fixed set of hidden "clones". Expectation-maximisation learns which clone follows which under each action; Viterbi training then prunes unused clones. A clone stands for a sensation in a particular sequential context, so when the experimenter plots a clone's activity on a floor map, it looks like a place cell.

  • Maps from aliased egocentric input. CSCGs learned the topology of 2D rooms and 3D environments from random walks, with no location input and no Euclidean assumptions. Clones also came to represent heading, which is never sensed directly.
  • Stitching and separating. Overlapping episodes collected at different times were stitched into one global map. Experience from disjoint mazes was kept as separate maps, and the right map is recalled by inferring the hidden state.
  • Planning as replay. To reach a remembered goal, messages are passed forward from the current clone and backward from the goal clone, which the authors liken to hippocampal replay. Blocking a route means editing one link of the graph.
  • Schemas. Keeping the learned transition graph fixed and relearning only the emissions let the agent find a shortcut through unvisited rooms in a new environment that shared the old one's structure.
  • Place fields as a visualisation. In the paper's words, many "diverse fascinating hippocampal phenomena might be artifacts of Euclidean place field mapping." The agent itself never computes place fields.

Testable predictions

  • Uniqueness, not visual change, controls field size. A floor with a repeating checkerboard should give larger central place fields than a floor with a random pattern, because the checkerboard makes many sequences look the same (Fig. 4F).
  • Local landmarks decide which fields remap. When a room with landmarks on one side is elongated, fields whose local sequence is anchored by two landmarks should stay put. Fields anchored to a landmark and the moved wall should stretch (Fig. 4G).
  • Connectivity changes show up in planning, not in fields. Locking doors should alter replay and route choice while place fields stay put.
  • Grid cells are helpful but optional. They act as extra sensations that speed learning in featureless areas, consistent with place fields surviving grid disruption.

Limits the authors note

  • Animals form place fields with far less experience than the model needs; reusing schemas may close that gap.
  • Remapping to new objects, active exploration, reward learning and automatic graph editing after surprise are not yet modelled.
  • The work is computational. Clones might be neuronal assemblies and EM resembles spike-timing-dependent plasticity, but no circuit implementation is claimed.
  • Unlike the Kantian view that space and time come first, the results support a Leibnizian view: only sequential order needs to be built in.

Replay: mental time travel in a rat

The rat shuttles between two reward wells while 16 place cells (sorted by field position) fire in order. At each well it stops, and during sharp-wave ripples the same sequence is replayed about 20 times faster: backward on arrival, as if reviewing the path that led to reward, and forward before leaving, as if planning (Foster & Wilson 2006; Diba & Buzsáki 2007). In a sequence model, recall and planning are both just running the learned graph.

Timing rules

Frequency, calcium and the sliding threshold

Low-frequency stimulation causes a modest, prolonged Ca²⁺ rise that activates phosphatases (calcineurin, PP1) and depresses the synapse. High frequency gives a large, fast Ca²⁺ rise that activates kinases (CaMKII) and potentiates it. The crossover point slides with the neuron’s history (the BCM rule; Bienenstock, Cooper & Munro 1982). After high activity, LTP becomes harder; after deprivation, easier. This is metaplasticity (Abraham & Bear 1996).

Foundations

Three levels of explanation

David Marr argued that a brain system is understood only when it is explained at three levels. His 1971 “Simple memory: a theory for archicortex” was the first mathematical theory of the hippocampus: sparse codes in a recurrently connected CA3 store events from a single exposure and recall them from a fragment. Nearly every model on this page descends from it.

Computational
What problem is solved, and why? Store episodes after one exposure; keep similar ones apart; recall from partial cues; map space and time; replay for consolidation.
Algorithmic
Which representations and procedures? Sparse expansion (DG), attractor dynamics (CA3), comparison of prediction and input (CA1), Bayesian decoding, successor maps, latent sequences.
Implementation
Which neurons, synapses and rhythms? Mossy-fibre detonators, recurrent collaterals with NMDA-receptor plasticity, PV-cell gamma, theta from the septum, plateau-driven BTSP.

Attractor memory: capacity and synapse loss

A 200-unit Hopfield network stores P random patterns with a Hebbian rule, then recalls each from a noisy cue. Recall stays near perfect until the load α = P/N approaches ≈0.14, then collapses (Amit, Gutfreund & Sompolinsky 1985). Removing synapses, as in Alzheimer’s disease, lowers the collapse point: memory fails abruptly, not gradually. The grey curve is the intact network.

Excitation–inhibition dynamics: rhythm and seizure

Reading out place: Bayesian decoding

Each cell fires Poisson spikes at a rate set by its Gaussian field on a 1 m track. The posterior over position (top, accent) is computed from the spike counts alone (Zhang et al. 1998). Error (bottom) falls roughly as 1/√N; broader fields and shorter windows blur the code. Unstable or broadened fields in ageing and Alzheimer models act like turning these dials down.
