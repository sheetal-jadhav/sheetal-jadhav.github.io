Papers I–III · voltage clamp

Holding the voltage separates the two currents

Under voltage clamp the membrane potential is stepped and held, so each gate relaxes exponentially to its new steady state. Sodium conductance rises and then inactivates. Potassium conductance rises with a delay, following n⁴, and stays up.

Paper IV · sodium inactivation

A conditioning step sets how much sodium current is available

The membrane is held at a conditioning voltage long enough for h to settle, then stepped to a fixed test depolarisation. The peak sodium current, normalised to its largest value, traces the steady-state inactivation curve h∞(V).

Putting it together

From five papers to one spike

The papers form a single argument. Measure the currents with the voltage held fixed. Separate them by ion. Describe each with gating variables. Then, with nothing left to adjust, compute the action potential and see whether it matches the real one.

Why it still matters

Hodgkin and Huxley showed that excitability follows from two voltage-dependent conductances and the membrane capacitance, with no extra mechanism. Their gating particles predicted voltage-gated ion channels before anyone could see one. Single channels were first recorded with the patch clamp (Neher and Sakmann, 1976). The same conductance-based form, I = C dV/dt + Σ g(V − E), underlies most biophysical neuron models in use today. Hodgkin and Huxley shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with John Eccles.

Equations, constants and numerics

Model (Paper V, 1952 sign convention)

I = CM dV/dt + ḡKn4(V − VK) + ḡNam3h(V − VNa) + ḡl(V − Vl)
dx/dt = αx(1 − x) − βxx,  x ∈ {m, h, n}
αn = 0.01(V + 10) / [exp((V + 10)/10) − 1]
βn = 0.125 exp(V/80)
αm = 0.1(V + 25) / [exp((V + 25)/10) − 1]
βm = 4 exp(V/18)
αh = 0.07 exp(V/20)
βh = 1 / [exp((V + 30)/10) + 1]
all α, β × φ,  φ = 3(T − 6.3)/10
(a/2R2) ∂²V/∂x² = CM ∂V/∂t + Iipropagated case

V is the displacement from the resting potential in mV, with depolarisation negative. Inward current is positive, which the constants imply: with VNa = −115 mV the sodium current comes out positive. The modern display flips both voltage and current, so that depolarisation and outward current are positive. α and β are in ms⁻¹, t in ms, I in µA/cm², g in mS/cm². At V = −10 and V = −25 the expressions for αn and αm are 0/0 and take their limits, 0.1 and 1.0 ms⁻¹.

Numerical methods

  • Space-clamped runs (experiments 1–2): fourth-order Runge–Kutta, Δt = 0.01/φ ms (0.01 ms at 6.3 °C).
  • Voltage clamp (3–4): exact solution. With V held fixed each gate follows x(t) = x∞ − (x∞ − x₀)e−t/τ, with x∞ = α/(α+β) and τ = 1/(α+β). The clamp is ideal: no capacitive transient and no series resistance.
  • Cable (5): second-order central differences in space, Δx = 0.25 mm over 6 cm (241 nodes), sealed ends. V is advanced by forward Euler with Δt = 0.2 Δx²/D, where D = a/(2R₂CM). The gates are advanced by the exact exponential (Rush–Larsen) update, with rates tabulated every 0.01 mV and linearly interpolated. The stimulus is 3 mA/cm² for 0.1 ms over the first 2 mm.
  • Velocity is the inverse slope of a least-squares fit of the time each node passes 50 mV depolarisation, against position, for 1.5–4.5 cm. Halving Δx moves it by less than 0.1 m/s (0.5 → 0.25 → 0.125 mm: 18.55, 18.68, 18.72 m/s at the paper’s settings).
  • Spike criterion: depolarisation beyond 50 mV. Thresholds are found by bisection to 0.01 mV.

What the model leaves out

  • Gating is deterministic. There is no channel noise, so firing near threshold is perfectly repeatable.
  • Concentrations are fixed. There is no Na⁺/K⁺ accumulation or pump current, and repetitive firing runs indefinitely.
  • Reversal potentials are constants, not computed from the Nernst equation.
  • The cable is a uniform, unmyelinated cylinder with no extracellular resistance.
  • The modern voltage axis (Vm = −65 − V) relabels the display only. A resting potential of −65 mV is a common modern choice, not a value from the 1952 papers.
